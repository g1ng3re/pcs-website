Four properties or more: how lenders assess a portfolio landlord

6 min readLandlords

Buying your fourth mortgaged rental property changes how every lender looks at you. Up to that point, each application is mostly about the property in front of them. From then on, it is about everything you own.

What makes you a portfolio landlord

Most lenders define a portfolio landlord as someone with four or more mortgaged buy-to-let properties. They count every mortgaged property, with every lender, not just the ones they hold. Many also count properties held through limited companies you control.

The rules come from the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), which supervises lenders. Since 2017 it has expected lenders to assess portfolio landlords more thoroughly than landlords with one or two properties.

What lenders ask for

  • A property schedule listing every property, its value, monthly rent, mortgage balance, lender and monthly payment.
  • Your assets and liabilities, including personal borrowing.
  • A cash flow forecast for the portfolio.
  • A short business plan explaining your strategy and how the next purchase fits it.
  • Your tax position, because tax on rental income affects how much profit is really left.
  • Your experience as a landlord.

None of this is unusual for an established landlord. What catches people out is having it in a format a lender can read quickly.

Rental cover on the new property

Every buy-to-let application is tested on rental cover, sometimes called the interest cover ratio (ICR). The lender checks that the expected rent covers the mortgage payment by a set margin, typically between 125% and 145%, calculated at a stress rate higher than the rate you will actually pay.

The margin often depends on your tax position. Lenders tend to ask more of higher rate taxpayers buying personally than of limited companies.

Rental cover across the whole portfolio

This is the part that is new at four properties. Many lenders also run a background test on the existing portfolio, checking that the rent across all your properties covers all your mortgage payments by their required margin.

That means one weak property can affect the next purchase, even if it has nothing to do with it. A property let below market rent, a long void or an expensive variable rate can be enough to pull the portfolio under the line.

Check before you offer. It is much easier to fix a weak spot, for example by refinancing one property or adjusting the structure of the next purchase, before you have agreed a price than during an application.

Ways to strengthen your position

  • Keep a live property schedule. Update it whenever a rent, rate or balance changes, so it is ready when you need it.
  • Watch your end dates. Several fixed rates ending at once can push the whole portfolio onto higher rates and weaken the background test.
  • Review weak properties. A property that drags the portfolio down may be worth refinancing, re-letting or, in some cases, selling.
  • Think about structure. Personal and limited company ownership are assessed differently. Your accountant should advise on the tax side.
  • Use top slicing where it fits. Some lenders will let surplus personal income make up a rental shortfall on a single property. Not all lenders offer this, and the rules vary.

Why the lender matters more at this stage

Lenders differ widely on how they treat portfolio landlords: how they run the background test, which properties they count, what stress rate they use and whether they consider top slicing. A case that one lender declines can fit another comfortably.

The short version

At four mortgaged properties, lenders stop looking at the purchase and start looking at the portfolio. Keep your schedule up to date, know which properties are weakest, and have the portfolio reviewed before you commit to the next purchase.
