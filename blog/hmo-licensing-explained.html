HMO licensing explained: what landlords need before they let

7 min readLandlords

An HMO can produce a stronger rental yield than a single let. It also comes with more rules, and most of them sit with your local council rather than your lender. Get the licensing wrong and the problem is not just a fine. It can stop a mortgage completing, or put the rent you have already collected at risk.

This guide covers the rules in England. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland run their own systems.

What counts as an HMO

A property is usually a house in multiple occupation (HMO) when at least three tenants live there, they form more than one household, and they share a kitchen, bathroom or toilet. A household is a single person, a couple, or members of the same family living together.

So three friends sharing a house is an HMO. A couple with their two children is not, because they are one household.

The three types of licence

Mandatory licensing

Every large HMO in England needs a licence from the council. A large HMO is one let to five or more people who form more than one household. The size of the building and the number of storeys do not change this.

Additional licensing

Councils can extend licensing to smaller HMOs in all or part of their area. A three or four person HMO that needs no licence in one borough may need one in the next.

Selective licensing

Some councils also license ordinary rented homes, not just HMOs, in designated areas. If your property sits in one of those areas, a licence may be needed whatever its size.

Check the council, not the rule of thumb. Licensing schemes are set borough by borough and change over time. Your council's website lists the schemes in force and what each one requires.

Planning permission and Article 4

Licensing and planning are separate. A licence says the property is safe and well managed. Planning permission says it can be used as an HMO at all.

In many areas, turning a family house into a small HMO for up to six people does not need planning permission. Where the council has made an Article 4 direction, that freedom is removed and you need permission first. HMOs for more than six people normally need planning permission wherever they are.

Lenders and their valuers will ask about planning as well as licensing, so it is worth having both settled before you apply.

What a licence usually requires

  • Room sizes. National minimums apply to bedrooms in licensed HMOs: 6.51 square metres for one adult and 10.22 square metres for two. Rooms under 4.64 square metres cannot be used for sleeping.
  • Fire safety. Typically fire doors, a linked alarm system and a protected escape route. The council sets the standard for your property.
  • Facilities. Enough kitchens, bathrooms and toilets for the number of people living there.
  • A fit and proper landlord. The council checks the licence holder and the manager.
  • Waste and management. Arrangements for refuse, repairs and contact details for tenants.

A licence normally lasts up to five years, and the council can attach conditions such as a maximum number of occupants.

What happens if you get it wrong

Letting an HMO that needs a licence without one is a criminal offence. Councils can prosecute or issue civil penalties, and tenants can apply for a rent repayment order to reclaim rent they paid while the property was unlicensed. It can also leave you in breach of your mortgage and your insurance.

How licensing affects your mortgage

Most HMO lenders will ask whether the property is licensed, or whether an application is in progress, and some will want to see the licence itself. They will also look at the number of bedrooms, the shared facilities and whether the property meets local standards. A licence that does not match the way the property is actually let can hold up a valuation.

The short version

Find out which licensing schemes your council runs, check whether an Article 4 direction applies, and make sure the property meets the room size and fire safety standards before you let it. Then speak to a broker before you apply for finance, so the licence, the planning position and the mortgage all line up.
